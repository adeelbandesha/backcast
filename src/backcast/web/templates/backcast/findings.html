Findings

Every member of the S&P 500, backtested against the naive forecast at three horizons. Each stock also ran on placebo copies of itself, on which no model can have a real edge. The placebos show how often the test finds a winner by luck alone.

What it found

No model beat naive by enough to matter. In the run on closes to {{ study.end }}, the SPA test picked out 8 winners among 498 stocks at one day, 2 at five days and 2 at twenty days. On the placebos, where every win is luck, the five draws produced 1 to 5 winners at one day, 0 to 7 at five days and 2 to 20 at twenty days.

At five and twenty days the real stocks did no better than chance. At one day, 8 is more than any placebo draw produced, so something is there, but it is small: on those 8 stocks the best model's error was 0.1% to 0.5% below naive's. The test also turned out conservative. On the placebos, where every win is false, it found a winner on 0.4% to 1.7% of stocks on average, well under the 5% a threshold of 0.05 suggests, which is why the placebos are the yardstick here.

The one-day result has a plain cause. Over these two years, daily moves tended to reverse a little the next day: the median correlation between one day's return and the next was −0.024, and 70% of stocks had a negative one. Theta and ETS lean slightly against the last move, which pays when moves reverse. Theta's error was below naive's on 59% of the real stocks and 33% of the placebos, whose random signs remove any reversal. At the median stock it was only 0.02% below. The pattern is real, and too small to use.

The bigger wins look like trends rather than skill. Valero won at one day and at twenty, both times through Drift, which bets that the past trend carries on, and its price roughly tripled over the period. At twenty days Drift's error was 11% below naive's when the close sat above its {{ trend_days }}-day average and 2% above it when the close sat below. The other twenty-day winner, Phillips 66, is also an oil refiner.

LightGBM, the one machine-learning model, did worst: at the median stock its error was 2% to 3% above naive's, and the Diebold-Mariano test didn't find it ahead on any stock at any horizon. Survivorship bias should have helped Drift, since the stocks still in the index did well enough to stay, and that test found it ahead on one stock in all. For these large US stocks over these two years, today's price was as good a forecast as any of the models made.

How the study was run

The universe is the {{ notes.universe.members }} members of the S&P 500 listed on Wikipedia on {{ notes.universe.retrieved }} (this revision). {{ study.tested }} of them were tested at one horizon or more; the first table counts each horizon. Every stock gets five years of daily closes ending {{ study.end }} and the same backtest as the rest of Backcast: a forecast from each of the last {{ study.origins }} trading days, models re-estimated every {{ study.refit }}, scored on prices they had not seen. The method page has the details.

A placebo replays a stock's own daily moves with random signs. Every move keeps its size, so the volatility, its clustering and the fat tails are real; only the direction is a coin toss, and no model can forecast that. Each date gets one coin toss for all stocks, which keeps stocks that moved together moving together.

It also hands the whole market a single random path. If that path happens to trend, trend bets win on many placebos at once, so one draw could make chance look generous or stingy. The placebo was drawn {{ study.seeds|length|apnumber }} times, from the seeds {{ study.seeds|join:", " }}.

Limits

  • The universe is today's index. Companies that fell out of it over the five years are missing, and the ones left did well enough to stay. This survivorship bias favours stocks that rose, and with them Drift.
  • Stocks move together, so their results are not independent, and a share of wins has no textbook margin of error. The spread across the placebo draws is the stand-in. With this few draws it shows roughly how far chance moves the share, not its exact limits.
  • These are two particular years of forecasts, and the next two could go differently.
