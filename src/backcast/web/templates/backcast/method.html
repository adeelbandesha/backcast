Method

Backcast asks one question of every stock: did any model forecast its price better than assuming nothing changes? This page describes exactly how that is measured, and where the measurement stops.

The benchmark

The naive forecast says the close in h trading days will equal today's close. For prices that behave like a random walk it is the best forecast available, which is why it is the bar every model has to clear. Beating it consistently is rare.

Walk-forward windows

Backcast loads five years of daily closes and steps back from the latest one in jumps of h days, placing {{ windows }} forecast origins. The windows do not overlap, so each forecast is scored on a different future price. At every origin each model is fitted on all prices up to that date, with at least {{ min_history }} days of history, and forecasts the close h trading days later.

The closes are adjusted for stock splits but not for dividends. Dividend adjustment rescales every earlier price each time a dividend is paid, so the history would no longer show the prices people actually traded at. While a market is open, today's price is left out: it isn't a close until the session ends.

Because the windows are h days apart, the 1-, 5- and 20-day backtests cover the most recent {% widthratio windows 1 1 %}, {% widthratio windows 1 5 %} and {% widthratio windows 1 20 %} trading days.

Scoring

Every metric is computed at the horizon endpoint only, on prices the model had not seen.

Mean error
The mean absolute error, in the stock's currency.
vs naive
The model's mean error divided by naive's, shown as a percentage change. The leaderboard is sorted by it.
Windows won
The share of windows in which the model's error was smaller than naive's.
Direction right
How often the forecast moved in the same direction as the price. Windows where a model forecast no change at all are left out.
In {{ level }}% interval
How often the actual close fell inside the model's {{ level }}% prediction interval. Well calibrated intervals land near {{ level }}%.

Is the margin real?

For each model, Backcast takes the per-window difference between naive's absolute error and the model's, and runs a two-sided one-sample t-test on those {{ windows }} differences against zero. It's a paired test: both models face the same prices in each window, so a window that was hard for everyone doesn't count against either.

Five models are compared with naive, and every extra comparison is another chance for one of them to look good by luck. The p-values shown are adjusted for that with Holm's method, and a model only counts as a significant winner when its adjusted p-value is below {{ significance }}.

Trends

A significant result only means the model did better over these particular windows. Some models forecast the same direction almost every time: Drift, for one, always predicts the stock's long-run average move. In a period when the price keeps rising, a model like that beats naive window after window without forecasting anything. In a falling market it would lose just as steadily.

So Backcast checks the best model's forecasts. When it predicted the same direction in at least 90% of the windows and the price moved that way over the period, the headline says so. Below the headline you'll also find how much the price moved over the windows and how many of them it rose in.

Limits

  • Thirty windows is a small sample. Results can change from one week to the next.
  • Dividends aren't added back, so on the day a stock goes ex-dividend its price drops by roughly the dividend, and no model here forecasts that.
  • Only the price history is used: no volume, fundamentals, news, or other stocks.
  • Errors are measured on prices, not on trading returns. There are no transaction costs or positions.
  • Future dates follow the exchange's holiday calendar for most major exchanges. For the rest, they skip weekends only, and the chart says so.
